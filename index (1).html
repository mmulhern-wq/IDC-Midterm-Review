    Station 1 · Economic development

    GDP per capita, PPP and GNI

    ≈ 6 minutes

    You should be able to

    • Define GDP and GDP per capita, and explain why we divide by population.
    • Explain what purchasing power parity (PPP) fixes and why it matters most for poorer countries.
    • Classify countries as low, middle, or high income and explain the difference between GDP and GNI.
    GDPGross Domestic Product. The total value of new goods and services produced inside a country's borders in one year.
    GDP per capitaGDP divided by population. The average output per person. A big country produces more in total, so per capita lets us compare living standards fairly.
    PPPPurchasing power parity. Adjusts for the cost of living using a "basket of goods" (food, rent, haircuts) so a dollar means the same amount of stuff everywhere.
    GNIGross National Income. GDP + money citizens earn abroad (like remittances) − profits foreign companies take out.

    Station 2 · The critique

    What GDP can't see

    ≈ 5 minutes

    GDP counts every market transaction. It does not ask whether that transaction made anyone's life better. A car crash that leads to repairs, hospital bills and lawyers raises GDP. A family that cooks dinner together and goes for a walk adds almost nothing. That's the point of the reading we did on the "ideal GDP citizen."

    GDP is still useful. For a very poor country, growing output means food, clean water, schools and clinics, so focusing on GDP growth makes sense there. The problem comes when a rich country treats more GDP as the goal in itself.

    "The gross national product measures everything, except that which makes life worthwhile."Robert F. Kennedy, 1968

    Station 3 · Human development

    The Human Development Index

    ≈ 6 minutes

    The UN Development Programme created the HDI to give a fuller picture than income alone. It combines three dimensions: a long and healthy life (life expectancy at birth), knowledge (mean years of schooling for adults and expected years of schooling for kids), and a decent standard of living (GNI per capita, PPP). The score runs from 0 to 1.

    The income part uses a logarithm, so each extra dollar counts for less as a country gets richer, and anything above $75,000 adds nothing. That's diminishing returns built right into the formula. Countries are grouped as Very High (0.800+), High (0.700 to 0.799), Medium (0.550 to 0.699) and Low (below 0.550).

    Rich on paper, poor in life

    From Table 2.1 in the Sachs reading. A lower rank number is better. Countries above the line rank higher on HDI than on GDP; countries that slide down rank much lower on HDI than their wealth would suggest.

    Equatorial Guinea is the class example. Oil and gas made it rich on paper: GDP per capita topped $19,000 in 2008 and it ranked 41st in the world for income, yet 144th on HDI. In the 2025 report its life expectancy was still just 63.7 years with 8.3 mean years of schooling. The money hadn't reached most people.

    Station 4 · Paradox of plenty

    The Resource Curse

    ≈ 5 minutes

    You'd think finding oil would make a country rich. Often it does the opposite. The resource curse describes countries with abundant oil, gas or minerals that end up with slower growth, weaker institutions and more conflict than countries with fewer resources. Sachs connects it to inequality: when government revenue flows to a small circle of insiders, the resource becomes a source of corruption instead of development.

    The curse is not destiny. Norway, Canada and Botswana all have major resources and avoided the worst of it, mostly because strong, transparent institutions were in place to manage the wealth and share it (Norway's sovereign wealth fund is the classic example).

    Dutch DiseaseResource exports push up the currency's value, which makes the country's other exports (farming, manufacturing) too expensive to compete.
    Corruption & weak institutionsResource wealth fuels elite power and weakens democracy and transparency.
    ConflictCompeting groups fight over control of valuable resources.
    InequalityThe wealth goes to a small elite while most people stay poor.

    Station 5 · Distribution

    Measuring inequality

    ≈ 8 minutes

    Here, inequality means the gap in income within a country, not between countries. Averages hide it. Remember the man with his feet in the fire and his head on ice who says the temperature is "on average just fine." Or imagine a class where everyone's allowance is $0 except one person who gets $1,000. The average allowance looks healthy. Nobody's life does.

    Gini coefficient0 means perfect equality, 1 (or 100) means one person has everything. Roughly: 0.25 to 0.35 more equal, 0.35 to 0.45 moderate, 0.45+ more concerning. There's no "perfect" Gini.
    Lorenz curvePlots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of people. The further it sags from the line of equality, the higher the Gini.
    Quintile ratioAverage income of the richest 20% divided by the average income of the poorest 20%.
    Income share of top 1%How much of all income goes to the richest 1%. In class: Brazil 28.4%, Canada 13.6%, Finland 7.3%.

    Station 6 · Demographic, gender & environmental indicators

    People, gender and the planet

    ≈ 5 minutes

    Development isn't only about money. Demographic indicators tell us how a population is changing. The Gender Inequality Index (GII) looks at reproductive health (like the adolescent birth rate), empowerment (like the share of seats in parliament held by women) and the labour market (participation of men vs women). The Planetary pressures-adjusted HDI (PHDI) lowers a country's HDI based on its carbon dioxide emissions and material footprint per person, because development that wrecks the planet isn't sustainable.

    Crude Birth RateLive births in a year per 1,000 people.
    Crude Death RateDeaths in a year per 1,000 people.
    Natural Increase RateCBR − CDR, shown as a percent. Since the rates are per 1,000, divide by 10: (30 − 10) ÷ 10 = 2.0%.

    Trends: always ask WHY

    A number on its own tells you what. A trend over time tells you something happened. When you see a graph change, ask what happened in that country to make it look that way. For example, women's labour force participation in Afghanistan collapsed after the Taliban returned to power in 2021 and restricted women's work and education. The graph only makes sense when you know the history.

    Station 7 · Subjective wellbeing

    Can you measure happiness?

    ≈ 6 minutes

    Aristotle argued the purpose of politics is the wellbeing of the people. His word was eudaimonia: flourishing, or living well. Modern researchers split wellbeing in two. Affective wellbeing asks about recent emotions: did you smile yesterday, were you happy? Evaluative wellbeing asks you to judge your life as a whole. The evaluative kind is what countries get ranked on.

    The World Happiness Report uses the Cantril ladder, then explains the differences between countries with six variables: GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption. Sachs points out that social trust and relationships matter enormously, and that very materialistic people report less life satisfaction than people who value generosity.

    Station 8 · From the Sachs reading

    An unequal world, over time

    ≈ 4 minutes

    Urban vs rural. Cities tend to have higher incomes, better public services, more education and lower fertility rates. Rural families tend to be larger, partly because children help on the farm. Almost all future population growth is expected to be urban.

    Least-developed countries (LDCs). The UN's group of about fifty very poor, vulnerable countries, concentrated in tropical Africa and Asia. Many are landlocked (Niger, Mali, Chad, Afghanistan), which makes trade harder, or small islands exposed to storms and high shipping costs.

    Convergence vs divergence. Convergence means poor countries close the gap with rich ones; divergence means the gap grows. From about 1750 to 1950 the world mostly diverged (industrialization in a few places, then imperialism). Since the end of empire, convergence has gained the upper hand, though not everywhere.
